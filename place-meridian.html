Meridian, Mississippi

Meridian, the Lauderdale County seat in east Mississippi, is about 65 percent Black — a railroad city of some 21,800 Black residents and a regional hub for the Black coun

Meridian is a city of about 33,600 people in Lauderdale County, east Mississippi, close to the Alabama line — and about 65 percent of its residents are Black, making it one of the largest majority-Black cities in the state. Meridian was built by the railroads: it grew up where lines crossed in the nineteenth century and became east Mississippi's commercial hub, drawing Black families from the farm counties of Mississippi and Alabama into a city with Black business districts, churches, and institutions of regional reach. It remains the service center for a wide, heavily Black rural region. This article profiles Meridian's Black population in the census data: size and share, the railroad history that assembled it, and its present economic profile.

The Record

The 2020 census counted roughly 21,800 Black residents in Meridian, about 65 percent of the city's population — a majority that formed over the late twentieth century as the city's total population declined from its railroad-era peak above 40,000 and Black residents became a growing share of those who remained. Lauderdale County, at roughly 45 percent Black, surrounds the city with a large Black population of its own, and the counties beyond — Kemper, Noxubee, Lauderdale's neighbors in the Mississippi Black Prairie and the Alabama Black Belt across the line — are among the most heavily Black rural counties in America. Meridian functions as their city: the place their residents shop, receive hospital care, and attend community college, which gives its Black population a regional weight beyond its municipal count.

Meridian's Black community grew with the city itself. Founded at a rail junction in 1860 and rebuilt after wartime destruction, Meridian boomed as a railroad and timber town, and Black workers — in the rail yards, the sawmills, the foundries, and the service economy of a busy junction — established neighborhoods and institutions from the city's earliest decades. By the early twentieth century Meridian had a recognized Black business district along its downtown corridors, Black churches of regional prominence, and a professional class serving the Black counties of two states. The city was also a birthplace of American music: Jimmie Rodgers, the father of country music, was born in Meridian, and the city's Black musical traditions fed the same rail-borne exchange. What distinguishes Meridian in this reference is the durability of that structure — a Black majority that descends from the city's founding economy rather than from any single later migration.
